# How Did a Skincare Shopify Brand Increase AOV With Tiered Offers and Post-Purchase Upsells?

Most skincare brands on Shopify do not have a traffic problem. They have a repeat-purchase problem that shows up early, right inside the first order.

A customer arrives looking for one serum. They buy the serum. They leave.

That was the pattern for a Direct-to-Consumer skincare brand selling a range of face care products. Paid social was working, the product pages converted, and reviews were strong. But the average order contained close to one product, and the brand's margins were being squeezed by shipping and packaging costs on small orders.

The brand did not add a new acquisition channel. Instead, it changed two moments in the buying journey: the point where a customer is choosing quantity, and the moment immediately after payment.

Over roughly six weeks, average order value moved from about **₹880 to ₹1,240**, and average items per order moved from **1.3 to 1.8**.

Here is what was changed, and what other skincare and personal care brands can take from this **Shopify AOV case study**.

## **Why Do Skincare Brands Struggle With Low Average Order Value?**

Skincare has a specific problem that FMCG and fashion do not share in the same way: customers are cautious.

A first-time buyer is not sure the product will suit their skin. So they buy the smallest possible commitment, which is usually one unit of one product.

That caution is reasonable. But it creates three commercial problems at once:

- Shipping and packaging cost is spread across a single low-value item.
- The customer experiences only one product, so the brand has fewer chances to earn a second purchase.
- Customer acquisition cost has to be recovered from a thin first order.

The store was effectively asking every new customer to make the hardest possible decision, alone, with no guidance on quantity or routine.

## **What Did the Data Show Before Any Changes Were Made?**

Before changing anything, the brand looked at what its orders actually contained.

Three findings mattered:

- Around seven in ten orders contained a single product.
- The bestselling serum appeared in most orders, but rarely alongside the moisturiser that was designed to be layered over it.
- Customers who bought two or more units of the same product were noticeably more likely to order again later.

That third point reframed the objective. This was not only about a bigger first order. Customers who bought enough product to finish a full skincare cycle were more likely to see a result, and customers who saw a result came back.

So the goal became: **help customers buy a complete routine, or enough of one product to actually use it.**

## **How Did Tiered Quantity Offers Change Buying Behaviour?**

The first change was introducing tiered quantity offers on the bestselling products.

Instead of a single "Add to cart" button with one price, the product page presented a small set of clear choices:

- **1 unit** — try it
- **2 units** — most popular, better value per unit
- **3 units** — best value per unit, free shipping

The middle option was deliberately positioned as the default recommendation, with the per-unit saving stated in plain language rather than buried in a percentage.

This works in skincare for a reason that is easy to miss: a single bottle of serum often does not last long enough for a customer to judge the result. A two or three month supply matches how the product is genuinely meant to be used.

The offer was therefore not a discount dressed up as a bundle. It was a quantity that matched the product's own usage cycle.

Shopify's guidance on quantity breaks and volume discounts makes a similar point: tiered pricing tends to perform best when the tier reflects a real consumption pattern, rather than an arbitrary number chosen to hit a revenue target.

## **Why Was the Second Tier the One Most Customers Chose?**

Presenting three options changes the question the customer is answering.

Without tiers, the question is: **should I buy this or not?**

With tiers, the question becomes: **how much of this should I buy?**

That is a much easier question for someone who has already decided they want the product. Most customers avoided both extremes and selected the middle option, which was exactly the tier the brand had designed to be the sensible choice.

Two details mattered in making that work:

- The saving on the middle tier was visible without any calculation.
- The top tier existed partly to make the middle tier feel reasonable.

Removing the third tier would have made the second tier look like the expensive option instead of the balanced one.

## **How Did a Post-Purchase Upsell Add Revenue Without Hurting Conversion?**

The second change happened after checkout was complete.

Once payment went through, the customer saw a single one-click offer on the thank-you page: the moisturiser designed to be layered over the serum they had just bought, at a modest first-time price, added to the same order and the same shipment.

No card details had to be re-entered. No new checkout. Declining took one tap.

This is the part that made it safe to try. A pre-purchase popup risks distracting a customer who is midway through paying. A post-purchase offer cannot damage the conversion rate of the order it follows, because that order is already complete.

For a cautious skincare buyer, the timing also helps. The hesitation about whether to trust the brand has just been resolved by the decision to buy. Adding one complementary product to an order that already exists is a far smaller commitment than adding it before the first purchase.

A meaningful share of customers accepted, and the offer added incremental revenue on orders that would otherwise have shipped as-is.

## **Why Did the Two Changes Work Better Together?**

The two changes solved different problems, at different moments, for different customers.

**A customer who already trusts the product** needed a reason to buy more of it. Tiered quantity offers answered that.

**A customer who was only ready to try one product** needed a low-risk way to expand their routine. The post-purchase upsell answered that, after the risk had already been taken.

Neither change asked the customer to browse the catalogue again. Both reduced the number of decisions rather than adding to them.

That distinction matters, because **cart upsell Shopify** features are often added in a way that increases friction: more popups, more banners, more interruptions competing for the same attention. The changes here replaced open-ended choices with a small number of clear ones.

## **What Were the Results of the AOV Optimization?**

Across roughly six weeks:

- Average order value rose from about **₹880 to ₹1,240**.
- Average items per order rose from **1.3 to 1.8**.
- Single-product orders fell from around seven in ten to under half.
- Shipping cost as a share of order value fell, because the same parcel now carried more value.

That last point is often more valuable than the headline AOV number. A larger order usually ships in the same box, so much of the additional revenue arrives with very little additional cost attached.

It would be wrong to attribute all of this to two features. The period also included ordinary variation in traffic mix, returning customers, product reviews accumulating, and seasonal demand. Any store measuring this should compare against a like-for-like period and watch refund and return rates alongside AOV, because a quantity offer that customers regret is not a win.

What the numbers do support is narrower and still useful: **the same volume of buying intent was converted into materially more revenue per order.**

## **What Should Other Skincare Brands Check First?**

Before adding any feature, look at what your existing orders contain.

**Ask:**

- What share of orders contain exactly one item?
- Does one bottle of your bestseller last long enough for a customer to see a result?
- Which two products are genuinely designed to be used together?
- Is there a quantity that matches a full usage cycle?
- Do customers who buy multiples reorder more often?
- Is your free-shipping threshold close enough to your average order to be reachable?

Then change one thing.

Add tiers to a single bestseller. Or add one post-purchase offer for the one product that most naturally follows it. Give it enough orders to produce a readable result before adding the next thing.

**Track:**

- Average order value
- Items per order
- Tier selection mix
- Post-purchase offer acceptance rate
- Refund and return rate
- Repeat purchase rate at 60 and 90 days
- Contribution margin per order

Repeat purchase rate belongs on that list. In skincare, a bigger first order is only genuinely valuable if the customer uses the product, sees a result, and comes back.

## How Can Shopify Skincare Brands Start Improving AOV?

If your store converts well but most orders still contain a single product, the next increase in revenue may not require any additional ad spend.

Start with the quantity decision on your bestseller, and the moment immediately after payment. Those two points touch every customer you already have.

**More traffic gives you more first orders. A better-designed order gives you more from every one of them.**
